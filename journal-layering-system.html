Weather · June 18, 2026 · 9 min read · By Rosalind Achebe, Product Development Lead

The three-layer system, rebuilt for hard effort in cold air

The layering model most of us learned was written for walking. Work hard in cold air and almost every assumption inside it comes apart.

If you have read anything at all about layering, you have read the same three sentences. A base layer to move moisture, a mid layer to hold warmth, a shell to keep wind and rain off. It is a sound model and we use it too. It rests, though, on one assumption that quietly fails the moment the effort changes: that the heat you are making stays roughly level.

Someone walking uphill under a pack makes a fairly steady amount of heat for hours at a stretch. A morning of intervals looks nothing like that. Warm up at 40% of maximum, hold 90% for four minutes, drop back to 50%, repeat five times, then stand in the cold for ten minutes while the heart rate comes down. Heat output across that hour moves by roughly a factor of five, and the moisture comes with it. What was right at minute six is wrong by minute twenty and genuinely unpleasant by minute fifty-five.

So the useful question is not what to wear. It is what to wear when, and which piece you are willing to carry once it comes off.

One crude rule, and it is the useful one

The twenty-degree rule is the one we keep coming back to, and part of its value is how crude it is. Dress for a temperature about twenty degrees Fahrenheit warmer than the thermometer says. If it reads 40, dress for 60. Standing at the door you should feel slightly underdressed. If the doorway feels comfortable, you are carrying too much for minute ten.

It works because most of your warming arrives inside the first eight to ten minutes of moderate effort. Almost every morning that turns uncomfortable was decided before anyone left the house.

Three adjustments to it. Wind is worth about ten degrees at anything above a light breeze, and considerably more once you are wet. Rain is worth about fifteen, because water pulls heat off you roughly twenty-five times faster than still air does. And easy days, recovery work, anything with long standing rests, need less adjustment than a hard session. Intervals with two-minute standing recoveries are really two mornings stitched together, and you have to dress for both of them.

Slightly cold in the doorway is correct. Comfortable in the doorway means overdressed by minute ten.

What a base layer is actually for

A base layer is not there to keep you warm. Its job is to move moisture off your skin and into the next layer, so that it can evaporate somewhere other than against your body. One that holds water is worse than wearing nothing under your mid layer, because wet cloth on skin during a standing rest is exactly how a cold morning turns into a bad one.

This is where cotton earns the reputation it has. Cotton holds up to 27 times its own weight in water and gives it back slowly. On a steady effort you may never notice. Add rest intervals, or a cold ten minutes walking back to the car, and a wet cotton shirt pulls heat out of you fast enough to turn an inconvenience into a real problem.

That leaves fine merino and technical synthetics, and they fail in different directions. Merino carries around 30% of its weight in moisture before it feels wet at all, which is why it stays pleasant across a much wider range of conditions, and it holds off odour well enough to wear for several days. It also dries slowly. Soak a merino base layer through and it stays soaked for a long time.

Synthetics move moisture faster and dry faster, which makes them the better answer on a hard morning where you will genuinely be wet. They do hold odour, unless the treatment is built into the yarn rather than sprayed onto the surface, in which case it survives the wash. That distinction is worth checking on any product page, ours included.

The mid layer is where it usually goes wrong

Most people treat the mid layer as pure insulation, reach for the warmest thing on the peg, and finish the morning wet from the inside. A mid layer worn under effort has a harder brief than a hiking one. It has to hold enough still air to keep you warm when you slow down, and move enough moisture not to turn into a swamp when you speed up.

Which is what grid fleece is for. The knit leaves open channels against the skin, so warm air sits in the pockets while moisture travels along the channels and out toward the surface. Insulation that behaves a little like a wick. Our Emberline Grid Crew is a 190 gsm take on it, deliberately at the light end, because the mid layer people buy is almost always warmer than the way they actually train.

A simple test. Twenty minutes into a steady effort you should be warm and the inside face should still be dry to the hand. If it is visibly wet at that point on a cold day, the layer is too warm. It is rarely too thin.

A shell is a wind decision before it is a rain decision

Most mornings that feel unbearable are wind mornings, not rain mornings. A windproof layer that makes no waterproof claim at all handles perhaps eighty percent of bad weather, at a fraction of the weight and the price, and it breathes far better while doing it. That is the argument for a wind vest or a light anorak living in your bag through the year.

Full waterproofing is a separate purchase with a real trade-off attached. Every waterproof membrane restricts moisture movement to some degree, whatever the breathability figure on the swing tag says. A 20,000mm shell like our Kestrel Shell will hold out sustained rain, and on a mild wet day you will still come home damp from the inside. That is physics rather than a fault, and anyone telling you otherwise has something to sell.

The working rule: buy the lightest waterproof that will genuinely keep rain out for as long as your longest morning, then dress lighter under it than feels right.

By temperature band

Starting points for a moderate to hard effort, assuming little wind and no rain. Take a layer off if it is blowing. Add one for an easy day.

  • Above 60 F. Singlet or tee and shorts, and nothing else. A cap if the sun is up.
  • 50 to 60 F. Short sleeve and shorts, with a wind vest carried rather than worn. This is the band where almost everyone overdresses.
  • 40 to 50 F. Long sleeve base layer and shorts, or a tee and half tights. Reach for gloves before you add anything to the torso. Hands give up comfort first.
  • 30 to 40 F. Base layer, a light mid layer, full length tights, a beanie and gloves. This is the band where grid fleece earns what it costs.
  • 20 to 30 F. Base layer, mid layer, wind shell, tights, beanie, gloves. Cover the surfaces facing the wind rather than adding thickness all the way round.
  • Below 20 F. The same, with a windproof panel across the thighs and something over the neck. Below about 10 F, treat every morning as an easy one.

The fifteen minutes nobody plans for

The common mistake is not made during the effort. It is made in the quarter of an hour afterwards, standing in cold air in wet clothing with no heat being produced any more. That is when people get properly cold, and it is why anyone who has been doing this a while keeps a dry layer within reach.

It does not need to be technical in the slightest. A dry cotton hoodie in the car does the job perfectly, precisely because you are not going to work hard in it. What matters is that it is dry and that it goes on inside a couple of minutes of stopping, before your core temperature starts to fall.

If you keep one thing from all of this, keep that. It costs nothing, we are not selling it to you, and it will improve more winter mornings than any jacket on this site.
