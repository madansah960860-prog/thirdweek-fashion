Home/Journal/What recycled nylon actually is

Materials · July 9, 2026 · 10 min read · By Tobias Marchetti, Materials Manager

What recycled nylon actually is, and where it falls short

Recycled content is the most printed claim in technical apparel and one of the least examined. This is what sits behind the number on our own labels, including the parts that do not flatter us.

When a product page says 100% recycled nylon, it is telling you where the raw polymer came from and nothing whatsoever beyond that. It says nothing about how the cloth was dyed, how it was finished, how far it travelled, or how many winters it will survive. Those four usually weigh more in the total footprint than the feedstock does, which is why recycled content on its own is a weak signal dressed as a strong one.

We use a great deal of recycled polyester and nylon, roughly 71% of our fabric volume by weight, and we think it is the right default. We also think you should be able to see exactly what you are and are not buying, so this piece is written to be used against us.

Where the material comes from

Two feedstocks are in common use, and they are not the same thing.

Post-consumer material comes from things that reached somebody and were then collected back: drinks bottles, discarded fishing nets, carpet. This is what most people picture when they read the word recycled, and it is genuinely keeping waste out of a landfill or out of the water.

Post-industrial material comes from manufacturing waste, meaning offcuts, spinning waste and off-spec yarn that never left the building. That is recycling in a real sense, and it is usually cleaner and more consistent to work with. It is also less impressive than it sounds, because a well-run factory has always had every reason to reuse its own waste. Presenting that as an environmental achievement is a stretch.

Both are legitimate. The trouble is that the word recycled on a label almost never separates them. Our line uses post-consumer PET for polyester and a mixture of post-industrial and post-consumer nylon waste, certified under the Global Recycled Standard, which requires chain-of-custody paperwork from the recycler all the way through to the finished cloth. Ask us for the certificate numbers on a particular style and they will come back to you.

Recycled content tells you where the polymer came from. It tells you nothing at all about dye, finish, freight or how long the thing lasts.

Mechanical against chemical recycling

Once the waste has been collected, there are two routes back to fibre.

Mechanical recycling shreds the material, melts it and pushes it back out as fibre. It takes less energy and it is far more common. The cost is that the polymer chains shorten a little each time round, so strength degrades with every cycle. In practice mechanically recycled polyester is blended with virgin material or spun to a lower specification. It also carries colour in from the input stream, which limits the shades it can be dyed to afterwards. That is why so much recycled cloth is black or near it. Dyeing downward is easy. Dyeing upward is not.

Chemical recycling takes the polymer back to its monomers and builds it again. What comes out is essentially indistinguishable from virgin material, can be dyed to any shade, and could in theory go round indefinitely. It is also energy hungry and, for now, expensive. Capacity worldwide remains small next to demand.

Nearly all the recycled nylon in outdoor and technical apparel today, ours included, is either mechanically recycled or made by depolymerising pre-sorted nylon 6 waste. Where a company does not say which route was taken, mechanical is the safe assumption.

The stage almost nobody mentions: dye

Dyeing and finishing usually account for a large share of a fabric's water use, chemical load and energy. Depending on the cloth and the process, wet processing can match or exceed fibre production in total impact. A fabric can be 100% recycled and still have been dyed at a mill with poor water treatment, which is a worse outcome than virgin fibre dyed carefully.

Which is why the mill a fabric comes from matters more to us than its recycled percentage. Eleven of our fabrics come from bluesign approved mills, meaning the chemical inputs, worker safety and water discharge have been assessed at facility level rather than roll by roll. That certification is harder to earn and tells you far more than a fibre claim, and it is advertised far less often because it does not compress into a line on a swing tag.

Dark colours matter here for a duller reason too. They hide the colour variation that comes with mechanically recycled feedstock, so a mill can run a higher recycled percentage in them. Our black and charcoal pieces genuinely carry more recycled content than our lighter ones. We would rather say that out loud than let anyone read it as a design preference.

Where recycled synthetics fall short

Three problems, stated plainly.

Shedding. Recycled polyester sheds microfibers in the wash exactly as virgin polyester does. Recycling the feedstock changes nothing about it. What helps is washing less often, washing full loads cold and gentle, using a filter bag, or fitting a filter to the machine. We would rather tell you that than let the word recycled imply the problem is handled.

The bottle question. Turning a PET bottle into cloth takes it out of the bottle-to-bottle stream, where it could have gone round repeatedly. Turning it into a jacket is, for now, a one-way trip. Still better than virgin polyester and a landfill, and still not the closed loop the pictures suggest.

Blends. A cloth that is 92% polyester and 8% elastane cannot be recycled fibre-to-fibre at commercial scale today, because separating the two does not pay. Nearly every stretch fabric on the market carries this problem, ours among them. The industry has been promising a fix for a decade. When one arrives we will use it, and we will say so on the page.

Three claims we will not make

  • That recycled means low impact. It means lower impact than the virgin equivalent, at the fibre production stage only, all else being equal. All else is rarely equal.
  • That a recycled jacket is carbon neutral. We have not completed a full footprint across our supply chain, so any neutrality claim from us would be arithmetic performed on a guess.
  • That buying a new jacket helps the environment. It does not. The lowest-impact jacket is the one already hanging by your door. If it can be mended, mend it, and if it happens to be one of ours we will do that for you at cost.

What is worth checking instead

Stood in front of two technical garments, the recycled percentage is among the least informative numbers on either page. These are more useful, roughly in order:

  • Is the water repellent finish PFAS-free? A genuine chemical hazard question, and most of the industry has now moved. Ours went PFAS-free across the range in 2024.
  • Is the fabric weight and full composition published? A company that hides the gsm is usually hiding something about how long the cloth lasts.
  • Is there a repair service, and does it have prices? A repair offer with a real price list is a commitment. A repair offer with no price attached is a press release.
  • Are the certifications at facility level? bluesign, WRAP, SA8000 and the Responsible Wool Standard tell you more about how something was made than any fibre claim can.
  • How long has the style been in the line? A piece sold unchanged for four years is a durability signal that no certificate captures.

By that last measure the Emberline Grid Crew is the piece we are quietly proudest of. Same cloth, same pattern, same pricing since 2021, restocked rather than reinvented every spring. It is the least glamorous form of sustainability available to a company like ours, and probably the most effective one.

The full account of our sourcing, certifications and limitations sits on the materials page. If you find a claim there we cannot put a document behind, write to us and we will change the page.
