Seasons · August 21, 2026 · 10 min read · By Delia Okonkwo, Coach and Wear Test Lead

Building a winter block that survives February

Almost nobody walks away from a winter block in December. They walk away in the second week of February, and the reasons rarely have anything to do with fitness.

I have coached winter blocks for eleven years now, and the pattern of failure is remarkably steady. The first three weeks go well because the plan is new. Weeks four to eight go well because the routine has set. Then somewhere around week nine, usually the first days of February, accumulated tiredness and weather and darkness combine and a fifteen-minute obstacle becomes a reason to stay in. Two missed mornings become a week. A week becomes the end of it.

What is worth noticing is that the people who finish are rarely the most driven. They are the ones who took the friction out beforehand. So this is about two things: the shape of the twelve weeks, and, more usefully, what you set up in advance so that the shape survives contact with a wet Tuesday.

The shape: three blocks of four weeks

Three four-week blocks, each one three weeks of building and a fourth at reduced volume. Twelve weeks altogether, which is roughly the run from the start of December to the end of February in the northern hemisphere.

Weeks one to four, rebuild the base. Volume rises gradually and intensity stays deliberately low. Most of it should be conversational. The purpose is to accumulate consistent easy miles and let tendon and connective tissue catch up, which they do far more slowly than the cardiovascular side. The common error here is running the easy days too hard, which quietly spoils everything built on top of them.

Weeks five to eight, add structure. One quality session a week, usually a tempo effort or long intervals held at controlled effort. Volume keeps creeping up. This is where fitness becomes visible and where most people feel best about the whole thing.

Weeks nine to twelve, sharpen and hold. Two quality sessions a week for most people, with volume flat or slightly down. This is the hardest stretch to be in, because the novelty has gone and the weather is usually at its worst. It is also where the work of the first eight weeks is consolidated into something you keep.

The reduced week at the end of each block is not optional and it is not a reward for good behaviour. It is where the adaptation happens. Cut volume by roughly 30%, keep the intensity but take away some of the amount of it. Skipping recovery weeks does not make anyone fitter sooner. It makes them injured slightly later.

The people who finish blocks are rarely the most driven. They are the ones who took the friction out in advance.

The friction audit

Before week one, sit down and write out every step between waking and starting. Not the running, the logistics. A typical list comes to eleven or twelve items: find the kit, find the other sock, check the weather, decide what to wear, fill a bottle, find the headlamp, find the reflective layer, get dressed, get out of the door.

Each of those is a small decision, and each one is a chance not to go. Willingness at 6 a.m. in February is finite and unreliable, and there is no point spending it on a sock. The aim is to make the morning ask for as few decisions as you can manage.

What works, roughly in order of effect:

  • Lay the kit out the night before, socks included. It sounds too small to matter. In our own wear test group it was the single most cited habit among the people who reached week twelve.
  • Decide the session the night before, and write it down. Choosing what to run while standing in the cold is how an easy morning becomes a missed one.
  • Own two of whatever you wear most. Out four or more times a week with one pair of shorts and one base layer, your laundry decides your week. Two of each takes that decision away from it.
  • Keep a dry layer in the car or by the door. Getting cold afterwards is most of what makes tomorrow morning feel unappealing.
  • Solve being seen once, properly. A headlamp with charged batteries and a reflective outer layer, kept in the same place every single time.

The kit decisions that actually matter

I am a coach rather than a salesperson, so let me be careful here. None of what follows makes anybody faster. It removes reasons to stay indoors, which is a different thing and, across twelve weeks, a more useful one.

Being seen is the one thing I will not bend on. If any part of the block happens before sunrise or after sunset, you need to be visible from in front and from behind. Reflective detail on a jacket alone does not do it, because headlights reach your legs first. Reflective on the parts that move, the ankles and the wrists, reads from much further away than reflective across a back, because motion is what the eye catches. Our Nightfall Reflective Shell weaves the reflective element through the cloth instead of printing it on top, which is a difference you only notice at wash fifty.

Hands and head first. Torso second. Most people put another layer on the body when they feel cold, when the problem is at the ends of them. Gloves and a thin beanie do more for comfort between 30 and 45 degrees than any second mid layer, and they do not cause the overheating that leaves you wetter than the weather did.

Wind protection before insulation on the legs. Legs make plenty of heat and rarely want insulating. What they want is a windproof front. That is the whole argument for a wind-panel half tight over a heavier full tight through most of a winter.

Something that dries overnight. Out on consecutive mornings, anything still damp at six will not get worn, and then the morning does not happen. That is the practical case for grid fleece over heavy cotton, and it is why our Emberline Grid Crew is the piece our wear testers most often ask for a second of.

What to wear at each temperature is set out properly in the layering piece, including the rule about being slightly cold in the doorway.

The three places a block breaks

The week somebody is ill. Someone in the house gets sick and five days disappear. The mistake is trying to win the missed volume back. Do not. Come back at roughly 70% of where you left off for a few days, then pick the plan up where it would have been. A block with one lost week and a calm return is worth far more than the same block with a panicked catch-up bolted onto it.

The one hard morning that goes badly. A session run at the wrong pace on tired legs will make anyone doubt the whole plan. It is one point in a hundred. Judge a block on the trend across four weeks, never on a Tuesday.

The day the weather is genuinely dangerous. Ice is the one condition where the correct answer is to move indoors or move the session. Running on ice is not toughness, it is a six-week injury with a short delay on it. Decide the indoor alternative in advance so the choice takes ten seconds rather than ten minutes.

What the block is really for

The point of a winter block is not the twelve weeks. It is where those weeks leave you standing in March, when the light comes back and you can start doing specific work from a base that took three months to lay down. Anyone who trains opportunistically through the winter and begins properly in spring spends six weeks getting back to the line the consistent one started from.

Worth holding onto in week nine, in the dark, in the rain, looking at a set of intervals you would rather not do. You are not out there for that session. You are out there so that March is available to you when it arrives.

Lay the kit out tonight.
