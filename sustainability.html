Materials and impact

This page is written to be checked, line by line, by anyone who wants to. Everything below is either a certification we hold, a number we can put a document behind, or a limitation stated plainly. Where the data does not exist yet, we say that instead of reaching for an adjective.

Longevity comes before everything else here

The quietest thing a garment can do is not need replacing.

Roughly 60% of the environmental cost of a piece of apparel is already spent by the time it reaches you, in fibre production, dyeing and finishing. That puts the largest lever we have in plain sight: make things that stay in use. Doubling a garment's working life roughly halves its impact for every year you own it. Everything further down this page sits below that in order of importance.

Which makes our first commitment an unglamorous one. Fifty wash cycles of testing before a fabric is approved. Patterns graded across the whole size range so nothing is carrying strain at the ends. And a repair bench that keeps pieces out of the bin for a few more seasons.

What the fabrics actually are

Recycled synthetics

  • Recycled polyester and nylon account for about 71% of our fabric volume by weight
  • The feedstock is post-consumer PET and post-industrial nylon waste, certified to the Global Recycled Standard with chain of custody we can show you
  • Recycled polyester takes roughly 30 to 50% less energy than virgin polyester, with the spread depending on the mill
  • Limitation: recycled synthetics shed microfibers in the wash exactly as virgin ones do. A filter bag or a machine filter is the only mitigation that works today

Wool

  • Every merino fibre in the line is certified to the Responsible Wool Standard, which covers animal welfare and land management together
  • It comes from grower groups in New Zealand and Australia, the same ones season after season
  • Micron counts are printed on the product page: 17.5 for the Featherweight Tee, 18.5 for the Ridge Half-Zip
  • Limitation: wool carries a higher land and water footprint than recycled synthetics. We use it where the warmth when damp and the years of wear earn that back, and not elsewhere

Cotton and cellulosics

  • All of our cotton is certified organic, grown without synthetic pesticides or genetically modified seed
  • Tencel lyocell comes from FSC certified wood through a closed-loop solvent process that recovers over 99% of the solvent
  • You will find them in the Loop Terry Crewneck and the Recovery Sweatpant, and blended into the Session Tee
  • Limitation: organic cotton is not a low-water fibre. It is a lower-chemical one. Those are different things and the label rarely separates them

Chemistry and finishes

  • Every water-repellent finish in the line is PFAS-free. The last of the changeover was completed across the range in 2024
  • Membranes are polyurethane rather than PTFE
  • Eleven of our fabrics come from bluesign approved mills, assessed at facility level rather than by the roll
  • Limitation: a PFAS-free finish needs reproofing more often than the chemistry it replaced. That is a real trade-off, and you should hear it from us rather than find it out in the rain

Where the work happens

Two cut-and-sew partners, one knitting mill, and our own benches in Asheville. We are a small customer at each of them, which limits what we can insist on, and saying so seems more useful than implying that a fourteen-person company sets a factory's standards.

Manufacturing partners

  • Outerwear and technical wovens: a family-owned factory in Da Nang, Vietnam, WRAP certified and audited every year by an independent third party
  • Knitwear: a knitting and finishing mill in Porto, Portugal, working under EU labour law and holding SA8000 certification
  • Slow Line: our own workshop in Asheville, North Carolina, where five made-to-order styles are cut and sewn one at a time
  • Audits: ask by email and we will send the full report rather than a summary of a summary

Freight

  • Bulk production moves by sea, never by air, even when the weather or the calendar makes that awkward
  • Volume 06 landed two weeks behind schedule for exactly this reason, and we let it
  • US parcels go by ground service by default. Expedited exists if you need it and we do not push it at checkout

Three ways a piece stays in use

A garment has three futures after you are finished with it. We would like all three to be better than a bin bag.

Repairs

  • Zip replacement, seam repair and patching on any Halden Field piece, whatever season it came from
  • Charged at parts cost plus return shipping, which usually lands between $18 and $45
  • Turnaround is two to three weeks from the bench in Asheville
  • A manufacturing fault is repaired or replaced free, with no time limit on it

Resale

  • We take back worn Halden Field pieces in usable condition and give store credit for them
  • Credit runs 20 to 35% of the original price, set by condition rather than by age
  • What comes back is cleaned, repaired where it needs it, and sold on at a lower price with the wear left visible
  • Write to us before you ship anything so we can confirm the credit first

End of life

  • A piece that is genuinely finished can come back to us for textile recycling
  • It goes to a regional recycler rather than onto a container bound somewhere out of sight
  • Mixed-fibre garments are downcycled into insulation and industrial felt, which is honest work but a one-way trip
  • Limitation: real fibre-to-fibre recycling of blends is not commercially viable yet. We are not going to describe downcycling as a closed loop

How it works in practice

Repair, resale, end of life

Programs like these only count if they are easy to start, so here is the whole path from the moment a piece stops being wearable, in the order it actually happens.

  1. Send a photograph first

    Write to us with a picture of the damage before you post anything. Most of what people send in turns out to be a zip slider or a bar tack, and we can usually quote the repair from the photograph inside a working day. That quote is the price you pay.

  2. Post it to Asheville

    Wash it first if you can. Return postage is yours and the repair is charged at parts cost, which is the $18 to $45 range on the card above. Nothing is sub-contracted. The same benches that cut the Slow Line do the mending.

  3. We repair it, or we tell you it is not worth it

    Two to three weeks is the usual turnaround. If a piece is past sensible repair we say so plainly and refund the quote rather than doing expensive work on a garment that will fail again in a season.

  4. If it has reached the end, it becomes credit or feedstock

    Still wearable and you are done with it: it comes back for 20 to 35% of the original price in credit, and we clean it, repair it and sell it on. Genuinely finished: it goes to our regional textile recycler, where mixed fibres become insulation and felt.

Before you send anything

  • Photograph the damage in daylight, close in
  • Say roughly how old the piece is and how it was used
  • Wait for the quote. It is free and there is no obligation attached to it

What we cannot fix

  • Widespread abrasion across a main panel, where a patch would just move the problem
  • Membrane delamination on a shell past its serviceable life
  • Anything altered by another workshop, since we cannot stand behind work we did not do

What it costs us

  • The repair bench does not make money and is not meant to
  • Resale competes directly with our own new stock, and we accept that
  • Both are cheaper than a customer replacing a jacket every second winter

Claims we do not make

Environmental marketing has a credibility problem, and most of it comes from sentences that sound absolute while meaning nothing you could check. These are the ones you will never read on this site, and why.

  • We are not carbon neutral. We have not completed a full scope 1 to 3 footprint. Until we have, any offset claim from us would be arithmetic performed on a guess
  • We are not zero waste. Cutting waste on wovens sits around 12 to 15%. We sell on what we can as offcuts and the number is coming down slowly, which is the only speed it comes down at
  • We are not plastic free. Most technical fabric is plastic. Polybags still travel with goods from our factories, though everything we send out from Asheville is paper
  • We do not use the words eco or green. Neither has an agreed definition, so neither carries information. They are decoration
  • We do not publish an improvement without a baseline. If we cannot show you the before as well as the after, the number stays off the page

Ask us for the documentation

Certification numbers, mill test reports and factory audit summaries go by email to anyone who asks, competitors and journalists included. If a claim on this page cannot be put behind a document, tell us and we will change the page rather than defend it.
